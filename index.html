Explainer · Canada–U.S. trade

“Elbows Up”: what it is, why it happened, and the best case on both sides

In early 2025, a hockey phrase became Canada’s shorthand for standing up to U.S. tariffs and annexation talk. Twenty months later, the trade war is still unresolved. This page explains the slogan, the history behind it, and gives the strongest version of the argument for and against the approach.

Where things stand (Oct 27, 2026) — and two corrections to the popular story

1. “Elbows up” has not been a straight line. Canada did not keep all of its 2025 counter-tariffs. On June 29, 2025 it rescinded its 3% digital services tax after President Trump suspended trade talks over it.[1][2] On August 22, 2025 Prime Minister Carney announced that Canada would drop its tariffs on U.S. goods covered by CUSMA, effective September 1, 2025; the tariffs on U.S. steel, aluminum and autos stayed.[3] CBC reported the duties had covered about $60 billion of U.S. goods;[4] EY put the repealed list at CA$30.3 billion in annual imports, with the steel/aluminum tariffs and motor-vehicle tariffs retained.[5] Critics called it “elbows down”; Carney replied that the game had moved to a different stage.[6]

2. The conflict re-escalated in 2026. After a tentative deal collapsed on August 21–22, 2026, the U.S. imposed new 50% tariffs under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 (effective August 22).[7][8] Canada answered with tariffs of 15%, 25% and 50% on roughly C$27.6 billion of U.S. goods, effective September 8, 2026.[7] The U.S. then banned about US$1 billion of Canadian products (alcohol, motorcycles, molasses and whey, among others) from September 29.[7]

CUSMA review. At the first joint review on July 1, 2026 the U.S. declined to agree to a new 16-year term. The agreement is not terminated: it remains in force until 2036, but the three countries must now hold a joint review every year until all three confirm an extension.[9][10][11] USTR opened public comment for the 2027 review (deadline Jan 12, 2027).[10] The publicly tracked bilateral negotiating rounds so far have been U.S.–Mexico.[12]

Latest talks. As of Oct 26, Reuters reports the two governments are working on a possible deal easing U.S. Section 232 tariffs on steel and autos (up to 50% on steel, 25% on autos), but sources on both sides say a broad deal is not imminent; Canada’s chief negotiator says “pens are down, and the doors are open.”[13][14] The U.S. midterms are November 3. On October 30 a further U.S. proclamation takes effect, removing some goods from the 50% list and adding others.[7]

1What “Elbows Up” is

The phrase. “Elbows up” is hockey language for protecting yourself in a scramble for the puck. Gordie Howe — “Mr. Hockey,” who played from 1946 to 1980 — was first known as “Mr. Elbows,” and his raised elbows are the image the slogan borrows.[15][16] Sources differ on whether he coined it: The Globe and Mail says he did; a biographer quoted by CBC doubts Howe ever used the phrase and says it “grew out of that legend.”[17][15] Commentators also stress that the move is defensive: you use elbows to ward off an opponent coming at you, not to start the fight.[18]

How it spread

Early 2025Manitoba Premier Wab Kinew says Canada “can’t be a punching bag, and we have to get our elbows up.”[15]
March 1, 2025On Saturday Night Live, Mike Myers opens his jacket to reveal a “Canada is not for sale” T-shirt and mouths “elbows up,” tapping his elbow.[17][19] He later said he was thinking of Gordie Howe and that the point was “Leave us alone… we can love Americans and not want to be Americans.”[19]
March 2025Protests at the U.S. Embassy in Ottawa and an “Elbows Up Canada” rally on Parliament Hill (March 9) put the phrase on placards; it spreads to T-shirts, caps, mugs and bumper stickers.[15][17][19]
March 2025In his first speech as Liberal leader, Mark Carney says: “We didn’t ask for this fight, but Canadians are always ready when someone else drops the gloves… In trade, as in hockey, Canada will win.”[16]
Spring 2025A Carney campaign ad shows him and Myers as “hockey dads,” Myers in a “Never 51” jersey.[19]
Aug 2025When Ottawa drops most counter-tariffs, opposition leader Pierre Poilievre says Carney’s “elbows have mysteriously gone missing.” Carney answers that there is “a time in a game… when you go hard in the corners with your elbows up,” and a time to “put the puck in the net.”[6]

Elbows up in practice: four layers

1 · Consumers. Buy-Canadian shopping, cancelled U.S. trips, avoiding U.S. brands. Abacus Data finds 77% of Canadians (Sept 2026) say they will avoid American products entirely or buy as few as they can — statistically unchanged from 78% in Feb 2025.[20]
2 · Provinces. Ontario ordered its LCBO to clear U.S. brands; the Distilled Spirits Council says all provinces but Alberta and Saskatchewan followed.[21]
3 · Ottawa. Counter-tariffs (2025, trimmed Sept 2025, re-escalated Sept 2026), the Buy Canadian procurement policy, and tighter steel quotas.[7][22]
4 · Diversification. Carney’s January 2026 Davos speech called the old rules-based order a “rupture”; Canada struck a deal with China (EVs, canola) and others — Carney said it is not a free-trade agreement.[23][24]

2Why it is: tariffs, annexation talk, and an unstable trade deal

The spark. After a late-November 2024 dinner at Mar-a-Lago, reports said Trump told Justin Trudeau that if a 25% tariff would “kill” the Canadian economy, Canada could become the 51st state. A Canadian minister at the table said it was a joke.[29][30] Trump then called Trudeau “Governor” of the “Great State of Canada” and described the U.S. trade deficit with Canada as a subsidy: “If we’re going to subsidize them, let them become a state.”[31] The sequence mattered: for many Canadians a trade dispute became a question of sovereignty — which helps explain why the T-shirt said “Canada is not for sale,” not “lower tariffs.”[19]

Why each side says it happened

U.S. position Canada discriminates against U.S. commerce: tariffs and quotas on U.S. cars (U.S. vehicle exports to Canada fell about 22%, or $5.6B, April 2025–March 2026 by the White House’s count), provincial bans on U.S. alcohol, and restrictive dairy quotas. The White House adds that only China and Canada retaliated rather than negotiate.[8][37]

Canadian position Canada says the U.S. broke the CUSMA bargain it signed in 2018, and that U.S. negotiators added last-minute demands in August 2026 — on auto tariff scope, on limiting Canada’s other trade deals, and on language and cultural protections like French-language streaming “discoverability.” U.S. officials dispute this (Lutnick said Carney “manufactured” the French issue); Canada’s negotiator disputes the U.S. account of last-minute truck demands.[34][38]

3Similar past events: what’s the same, what’s different

Canadian politics has a long history of wrestling with the same two questions: how much do we want to be integrated with the U.S., and what do we do when the U.S. uses that integration as leverage?

1911 · The reciprocity election

Canada votes against closer U.S. trade—and annexation fears decide it

Wilfrid Laurier’s Liberals proposed a reciprocity (free-trade) agreement with the U.S. It had little support in Ontario and Quebec, where voters feared an invasion of American products and eventually political annexation. Laurier lost to Robert Borden’s Conservatives.[39] The campaign was inflamed by U.S. House Speaker Champ Clark’s remark that he hoped to see the American flag fly over British North America.[40]

SameAnnexation talk from Washington turns an economic question into a question of national identity, and the voters side with “not for sale.”
DifferentIn 1911 Canadians were rejecting a deal offering more U.S. access; in 2025 they are resisting coercion of an existing integrated relationship. And the 1911 vote was muddied by another issue, the Naval Service Bill.[39]
1957–58 · Diefenbaker’s trade pivot

“Divert 15%” of imports from the U.S. to Britain

Soon after taking office in 1957, Prime Minister John Diefenbaker asked for a plan to redirect about 15% of Canadian imports from the U.S. to the U.K. A secret memo from the Prime Minister’s adviser said the pattern of trade reflected far more than commercial policy and that moving 15% “would require quite drastic action.”[41] Scholars conclude the policy ended up “pretty much where the Liberals had been” — increasingly reliant on the U.S. market.[42]

SameA leader tries to reduce dependence on the U.S. as a point of national policy. Today’s diversification push faces the same gravity: the BoC reports many firms say moving trade away from the U.S. is difficult because of transport costs to farther markets.[22]
DifferentIn 1957 diversification was a policy preference toward a single partner (Britain); in 2025–26 it is a reaction to tariffs and is spread across many partners. So far the U.S. share has fallen from 75.9% (2024) to 71.7% (2025) — movement, but nothing like a 15-point swing.[32]
1965 · The Auto Pact

A negotiated answer to the same industry now in the crossfire

Pearson and Johnson signed the Canada–U.S. Automotive Products Agreement on January 16, 1965 — a managed, integrated North American auto market with safeguards meant to preserve Canadian production and value added.[43] The logic echoes in 2026: the U.S. Section 338 proclamation objects to Canadian tariff-rate quotas that it says push automakers to invest in Canada rather than the U.S.[37]

SameAutos are where integration and national-economic interest collide; each country wants production on its side of the border.
DifferentThe Auto Pact was a negotiated, mutually agreed arrangement. Today’s fight is over tariffs imposed unilaterally and retaliation, not a bargain both sides have signed.
1972–1985 · The Third Option, FIRA and the National Energy Program

Canadian-initiated nationalism: reduce dependence on purpose

In 1972 Mitchell Sharp proposed a “Third Option”: lessen reliance on the U.S. through trade diversification, Canadian ownership and industrial strategy. It fed into the Foreign Investment Review Agency (screening foreign takeovers), Petro-Canada (1975), and the 1980 National Energy Program.[44][45] Brian Mulroney’s government replaced FIRA with the Investment Canada Act in June 1985, and the NEP was ended by the Western Accord on Energy, effective June 1, 1985.[46][45]

SameUse state power to reduce U.S. leverage over the Canadian economy — Buy Canadian policy, domestic steel quotas and diversification deals are cousins of the Third Option.[22]
DifferentThose policies were chosen at home, not in answer to a U.S. tariff war on Canada, and both were replaced or ended by 1985; the open question is whether today’s version will last.
1988 · The free-trade election

The great debate over sovereignty versus access

The 1988 election was fought over the Canada–U.S. Free Trade Agreement. Mulroney’s Progressive Conservatives won 169 of 295 seats with 43.01% of the vote; the Liberals (31.91%) and NDP (20.38%) — both opposed — together took about 52.3%.[47][48] The agreement took effect January 1, 1989 and is the ancestor of CUSMA.

SameThe sovereignty argument (“a deal with the elephant leaves us vulnerable”) is back. Opponents of the 1988 agreement made the sovereignty argument; supporters made the access argument.
Different1988 was a split country; 2025–26 is closer to a consensus on attitude — Angus Reid found 76% say walking away from talks was right, a majority in every province, though only 53% of 2025 Conservative voters agreed.[49] Disagreement now is about tactics and cost.
1982–2017 · Softwood lumber

Decades of U.S. trade remedies, answered with lawyers and settlements

Canada–U.S. softwood lumber disputes have run in repeating rounds: Lumber I (1982–83) ended without duties; a 15% tax under an MOU followed in 1986; a 1996 agreement set export fees to 2001; duties returned in 2001–05; a 2006 agreement lasted to October 2015; a fifth round began in 2016 and final duties were assessed Nov 2, 2017.[50] U.S. lumber tariffs rose again in October 2025.[7] The BoC notes lumber exports were about 20% below their 2024 average by February 2026, partly from “long-lasting trade disputes that predate recent tariffs.”[22]

SameThe U.S. uses trade law against a major Canadian industry; Canadian exporters, workers and governments absorb costs for years.
DifferentSoftwood was one product with a legal remedy process and recurring settlements; the 2025–26 conflict spans many sectors, mixes national-security and “discrimination” rationales, and arrives with annexation rhetoric.
2018–19 · Steel and aluminum, round one

“Dollar-for-dollar” retaliation that did work (eventually)

When the U.S. imposed Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum in 2018, Canada responded with surtaxes effective July 1, 2018 on about C$16.6 billion of U.S. goods — matching the value of affected Canadian exports — 25% on steel and 10% on aluminum and other goods.[51] Canada repealed them effective May 19, 2019, after the U.S. agreed to lift its tariffs.[52] In December 2024 Trudeau pledged to respond to new tariffs “as we did eight years ago.”[31]

SameSame tool (Section 232), same playbook (matching counter-tariffs, a political rally-round-the-flag), and in 2026 Canada again says it will match U.S. tariffs “dollar for dollar.”[49]
DifferentThe 2018–19 episode ended in roughly eleven months with a bargain; the current one has run about twenty months with the scope widened to autos, lumber and a broad 50% list — and with U.S. rhetoric about statehood.
2012 & 2025 · China versus Japan

When consumer anger becomes a state tool

In 2012, China’s Senkaku/Diaoyu dispute with Japan triggered a boycott: September sales fell about 49% for Toyota, 41% for Honda and 35% for Nissan in China.[53] In late 2025 China answered Japan’s prime minister’s Taiwan remarks with a travel advisory and a suspension of seafood imports; Reuters reported cancellations and sharply lower Chinese arrivals in Japan, while The Straits Times noted Chinese consumers kept buying Japanese goods this time.[54][55]

SameA consumer boycott aimed at a trading partner after a political insult, with real damage to specific sectors (cars then; tourism and seafood now).
DifferentCanada’s campaign began at the grass roots and is sustained mostly by consumers, with provinces joining — not a central government “advisory” aimed at a smaller neighbour. And Canada is the smaller economy in this fight, which changes the leverage maths.[38]
2025–26 · Denmark & Greenland

Another U.S. ally tries a boycott — with modest measurable effect

After U.S. pressure over Greenland, Danes downloaded apps to boycott U.S. goods.[56] A Coop survey found about one in four Danes cut U.S. grocery purchases but only about one in ten cut U.S. tech or entertainment;[57] Danish reporting found supermarkets noticed little change, in part because only a small share of Danish grocery products are made in the U.S. (about 1–3%), with effects visible in niches like U.S. wine.[58][56]

SameCitizen-led “buy local” politics aimed at the U.S. after territorial rhetoric, backed by apps and labelling.
DifferentCanada’s boycott is larger, longer and more government-supported (provincial shelf removals), and Canada buys far more U.S. goods than Denmark. That makes both the impact and the self-inflicted cost bigger.

4Steelman: the case for “Elbows Up”

Illustrative: the scale rocks because informed people weigh these arguments differently — it does not report a verdict.

This is the best version of the argument as its supporters would make it. The “strongest objection” under each point is the best reply from the other side.

1Backing down didn’t buy peace — and standing firm may be the only language Washington respects

Canada paid for conciliation in 2025: it scrapped the digital services tax on June 29 to get talks restarted, and the White House press secretary said Carney “caved.”[2] In August 2025 it dropped most counter-tariffs. Yet U.S. tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos and lumber persisted and a new 50% round came in 2026.[7] As late as August 18, 2026 an outline of a deal looked possible; former chief CUSMA negotiator Steve Verheul argues it would have been a “major dilution” of Canada’s principle of tariff-free CUSMA trade, with partial sectoral relief, demands well beyond tariffs, and a requirement to align Canada’s external tariffs with U.S. Section 232 decisions — which he says would breach Canada’s other trade agreements and amount to a “Fortress North America” where “one ruler” dictates.[38]

Strongest objection The U.S. says its August offer was real and Canada killed it with last-minute demands; Canada’s negotiator says the truck issue was raised all week.[34] We only have a partial view of what was on the table.

2The damage has been real but smaller than feared, and the economy is adapting

The Bank of Canada found that the industries facing sector-specific U.S. tariffs account for about 1% of Canadian output and employment and roughly 15% of exports; steel exports fell by half, but aluminum rebounded, copper exports were 40% above their 2024 average, and motor vehicles held roughly steady.[22] The PBO’s own note says non-energy export volumes in Q2 2026 were just 1.1% below its pre-tariff baseline — a smaller decline than its tariff model implies — and that exports to non-U.S. destinations were 51% above their 2024 monthly average by August 2026 (versus +10.5% to the U.S.).[36]

Strongest objection A chunk of the non-U.S. export growth is gold, and the PBO still projects a 1.4% hit to GDP after five years (see section 5).[32][33][36]

3It builds national unity, and may have helped Carney’s mandate and stabilised the federation

Three-in-four Canadians (76%) said walking away from the talks in August 2026 was the right call, a majority in every province including 65% in Saskatchewan and 67% in Alberta; 64% believe Canada will emerge stronger by reducing dependence on the U.S.[49] In October 2026 Alberta voters rejected starting a separation process by roughly 75% to 25%, widely read as a boost for Carney’s hand against the U.S.[35] The Abacus survey finds that the share who actually cancelled a U.S. trip rose from 32% to 52% of those for whom a trip was on the table.[20]

Strongest objection Unity at the polls isn’t the same as policy results; the same Angus Reid poll finds 38% of workers worried about their jobs and 89% worried about prices.[49]

4Diversification is overdue — the shock is forcing the structural change Canada never got to by choice

The U.S. share of goods exports fell from 75.9% to 71.7% in 2025, the lowest since the early 1980s.[32] The government says it has signed more than 20 trade and security deals across five continents;[49] the China arrangement allows 49,000 Chinese EVs at about a 6.1% tariff, with China expected to cut canola tariffs.[23][24] Verheul argues Canada’s “second main source of leverage” is exactly this de-risking, and that it must keep building “East-West” bridges.[38]

Strongest objection The BoC says many firms find diversification hard because of distance and transport costs, and the U.S. still takes roughly 70% of exports.[22][33]

5The costs fall on the U.S. too — and that’s where the leverage is

U.S. distillers lost nearly 1,000 jobs (3.5% of their workforce) between Sept 2024 and Sept 2025, the Distilled Spirits Council says, and Brown-Forman reported Canadian organic net sales down more than 60% in the first half of fiscal 2026.[21] Reuters reports tariffs on Canada have become a political liability in U.S. border states and that Republican candidates there are calling for an end to the trade war.[13] Verheul notes “a large majority” of U.S. economic actors support continued free trade with Canada and are becoming more vocal.[38]

Strongest objection Verheul himself writes that Canada “does not have ‘dominance’” in an escalating tariff war — “they can hurt us more than we can hurt them.”[38]

6It is defensive, proportionate and not a boycott of Americans

Howe’s elbows were for warding off attackers.[18] Carney’s own formulation is “we didn’t ask for this fight,”[16] and Mike Myers says “we can love Americans and not want to be Americans.”[19] The 2026 counter-tariffs were framed as matching, not exceeding, the U.S. list (C$27.6B), with a $7.5B support package, and Carney said Canada acted “reluctantly.”[34][49]

Strongest objection “Proportionate” is hard to square with the U.S. response of an import ban — in effect, retaliation against retaliation.[7]

5Steelman: the case against “Elbows Up”

This is the best version of the argument as critics make it — they include economists, parts of industry, and people who agree with the goals but not the tactics.

1The asymmetry is brutal: Canada can’t win a tariff war of attrition

Roughly 70% of Canada’s goods exports go to the U.S.[32][33] The PBO estimates the trade measures since early 2025 reduce real GDP by 1.4% after five years, with up to 185,000 fewer jobs (0.9%) if the tariffs are permanent — a scenario-based estimate, not a forecast.[36] It also estimates that the 2026 round lifted the effective U.S. tariff on Canadian goods from 5.3% to 7.7%, while Canada’s on U.S. goods rose from 1.6% to 3.0%.[36] A Globe and Mail commentary cites Bank of Canada and Peterson Institute models as showing that Canadian retaliation hurts Canada even more than it hurts the U.S., a price “worth paying” only if it changes U.S. behaviour.[59]

Strongest reply The PBO itself notes firms are adapting faster than its model assumes, and Q2 2026 exports were closer to baseline than expected.[36]

2Counter-tariffs tax Canadians — and didn’t visibly change U.S. policy

Business groups warned consumers would bear the costs; grocers said rural and northern communities were hit hardest;[60] manufacturers said they paid tariffs on U.S. inputs and again when exporting products made from them.[61] A CBC analysis in August 2025 said it was not obvious the retaliation was registering as much of an incentive in Washington.[6] Carney said retaliation was meant “to incentivize a negotiated settlement,” and CBC reported he argued the tariffs had become a sticking point in talks.[3][4] In 2026, the 15–50% counter-tariffs prompted a U.S. import ban on about US$1B of Canadian goods.[7]

Strongest reply Unifor’s Lana Payne: “Walking back counter-tariffs isn’t an olive branch. It only enables more U.S. aggression.”[62]

3Provincial liquor bans handed Washington a legal talking point

The Section 338 proclamations cite “discrimination” in three areas: auto tariffs/quotas, dairy quotas, and provincial removals of U.S. alcohol (“all but two Canadian provinces and territories have halted the purchase, distribution, or retailing of U.S. alcoholic beverages”).[8] USTR has called the bans a “serious concern,” and Greer has said lifting them is a key condition for a successful CUSMA review.[21] Ontario Premier Doug Ford says U.S. booze would return “in a heartbeat” for a fair deal for steel, auto and manufacturing workers.[14] So a symbolic, consumer-facing gesture became part of the U.S. legal case — while Ontario spent $8M storing unsold stock.[28]

Strongest reply The U.S. escalated on several fronts in 2025 and 2026 (steel, autos, lumber, copper), not just alcohol, so removing the bans might not have changed the outcome.[7]

4Consumer patriotism has a ceiling — price still decides

Leger (Sept 2026): price is the first factor in switching to Canadian products (67%); almost half would pick Canadian only if it cost the same or less, and just 9% would pay more than a 10% premium.[63] Abacus: six in ten still assume Canadian-made means paying more; the public cannot reliably tell which brands are Canadian (e.g., Costco is seen as American by 75%, Boston Pizza as Canadian by only 47%); only one in five will buy Canadian when it costs noticeably more.[20] And 72% recognise that many American-made goods contain Canadian ingredients or parts — a reminder of how integrated supply chains are.[20]

Strongest reply Abacus finds behaviour has risen on every measure it tracks, 19 months in — “the boycott hardened into a habit.”[20]

5The slogan oversells what’s deliverable — and could box in negotiators

CBC noted what matters now is “not how well we wield our elbows, but whether we put the puck in the net.”[6] A hockey metaphor can pressure leaders to look tough rather than strike the best deal: Canada has already faced “elbows down” attacks when it adjusted, and its chief negotiator acknowledged “in no case has any country been able to secure an agreement unless they agreed that U.S. goods were going to enter into their market tariff-free.”[6][34] Meanwhile, workers are paying the price: the industries hit hardest are steel, aluminum, forestry, and auto — the regions with the most to lose from a no-deal outcome.[36] Angus Reid cites industry experts predicting a no-deal situation could cost 0.4% of GDP and as many as 90,000 jobs.[49]

Strongest reply Canadians have told pollsters they would rather hold out: 69% said Carney showed “strength” by refusing terms he thought bad.[49]

6It may entrench the very dependence it aims to reduce — and the U.S. isn’t the only risk

Many “diversification” steps (e.g., the China EV/canola deal) carry their own geopolitical risk — Carney stressed it is not a free-trade agreement and China said it does not target the U.S., but Trump had threatened tariffs over it.[24] Canada says the U.S. sought to constrain its ability to strike other trade deals; USTR’s Greer says there was no such requirement in the text, only a discussion of aligning tariffs so Canada isn’t a “backdoor” for other countries’ steel.[34] Either way, “elbows up” is tied to one hard question: what would Canada give up for tariff relief?

Strongest reply Diversifying is lower-risk than concentrating 70% of exports in one market that has already shown it will use access as leverage.[32]
Common ground (editor’s summary, not a sourced finding)
  • The U.S. measures are the source of most of the damage; the debate is about Canada’s best response.
  • Diversification and domestic trade-barrier reduction are good ideas regardless of the trade war.
  • A deal must be good for Canadian workers; Carney says there are “very clear bottom lines, red lines.”[14]
  • Short-term pain is real; the disagreement is how much leverage Canada has to reduce it.

Bottom line. “Elbows up” is two things at once: a mood (nearly all Canadians want to resist being pushed around) and a bundle of policies (boycotts, counter-tariffs, diversification) whose costs and benefits are contested. Both the case for and against hold up on the evidence we have; people weigh the risk differently depending on how much they trust that the U.S. can be pressured, versus how much they fear being hurt by trying.
